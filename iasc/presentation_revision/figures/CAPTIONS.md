# English caption drafts for the five restructured main figures

**Figure 1. Illustrative loss of budget responsibility after a timeout.** A fixed scope has one cumulative action-budget unit and two distinct requests, A and B. In the hypothetical timeout-release chain, the sink commits A but the response is lost. Releasing the unit solely because the local outcome is unknown lets B use the same budget unit, producing two distinct effects even if each operation key is independently idempotent. Under retain-until-reconciled semantics, the unknown armed grant continues to hold the unit; reconciliation records A's effect and converts its held unit to committed expenditure. This is an **illustrative counterexample, not an observed failure of either full implementation**, and is not a new mechanism-ablation experiment. The committed unit remains spent under the cumulative-budget contract.

**Figure 2. Permission lifecycle and separate transaction boundaries.** A durable raw-request journal precedes atomic issuance of a complete permit, bound reservation and registry record. The arm commit creates a durable intent and irrevocable authorization. The separate service transaction makes the effect persistent; the subsequent local reconciliation transaction verifies the acknowledgement and atomically records the receipt, held-to-committed transition and completion. In the raw-request lifecycle experiment (S8), an expired unarmed permit can instead be atomically cancelled and released, after which later arm/dispatch is forbidden. An armed operation with an unknown outcome retains responsibility for its unit. The preissued-permit delivery experiment (S6) begins at the marked preissued boundary and does not evaluate issuance or S8's expiry-cleanup extension. This is an implementation schematic, not a claim that the full behavioral replay was sent through the independent service: S8 uses synthetic raw requests.

**Figure 3. Observed cleanup states in the saved raw-request lifecycle experiment.** Columns preserve the actual checkpoint identifiers for (a) `expired_unarmed_cleanup` and (b) `accepted_timeout_cleanup_recovery`; line breaks are typographic. Each cell shows the common count, or would show the minimum–maximum range, across both implementations and all three repetitions. All six observations have the same displayed count in this archive. The four rows are held units, committed cumulative units, independent sink effects and local receipts. The figure uses all five saved checkpoint stages for (a) and all four for (b), giving 54 checkpoint observations from 12 formal runs; the 12 current final database pairs were additionally read and agree with their final backups. In (a), expiry of an unarmed permit releases its unit, cancellation prevents its replay from dispatching, and other requests subsequently reuse the budget. In (b), the sink has committed but a delayed acknowledgement induces a client timeout; the missing receipt does not justify releasing the held unit. These are saved stage counts, not a wall-clock time series or a selection of favorable winners. The experiments use a cumulative alert budget of two; the illustrative budget of one in Figure 1 is a separate example.

**Figure 4. Prediction evaluation populations and external frozen policy regions.** (a) All six cohort–model combinations are shown. Squares denote pooled AUROC across observations; circles denote discordant-pair-weighted within-participant AUROC; diamonds restrict the within-participant calculation to alert candidates before capacity allocation. Within-participant and candidate bars are the saved conditional 95% participant-cluster intervals. Pooled values are shown as points only because no corresponding pooled interval is used here. The development and external cohorts contain 617 and 59 participants; 615 and 59 have both classes for the unrestricted within-participant calculation. Candidate-eligible counts for HGB, Logistic and History are 601, 596 and 543 in development, and 59, 58 and 50 externally. The three AUROCs concern different evaluation populations; their separation is not a same-estimand model-performance loss. (b) External frozen thresholds are 0.60, 0.58 and 0.43. The score-based review interval is [tau−0.03, tau+0.03], and alert eligibility is (tau+0.03, 1], before the ambiguity veto and capacity allocation. Shading marks the ambiguity gate [0.45, 0.55]. The Logistic review intersection is the singleton 0.55; the History action–gate union has width 0.10. No score, threshold or interval was refitted for this figure.

**Figure 5. Online pacing trades precision, coverage and budget utilization under the same hard ceilings.** All 12 combinations of two cohorts, three generators and two budget bases are shown: eight units per episode, or ceiling(8H/100) units using the protocol's planned horizon H. Values are pacing minus FIFO as proportion differences. Filled circles and bars show stored pooled estimates and paired conditional 95% participant-bootstrap intervals for (a) alert precision, (b) proxy-positive alert coverage and (c) alert-budget utilization. Open diamonds in (a,b) show the stored S7 participant-equal contrast on participants for whom both arms' metric is defined, with its paired interval. Across the displayed metrics and cells, these common-valid sets range from 576 to 615 development participants and 54 to 59 external participants; exact per-cell denominators and arm-only exclusions are exported in `fig5_plot_data.csv`. Utilization is pooled total alerts divided by total alert capacity; all 12 utilization intervals were already saved. The pooled and participant-equal intervals retain their respective archived 2,000-replicate analyses. No new bootstrap, model fit, or retrospective filling of unused budget was performed. Proposals, operating points and planned horizons remain fixed; identical ceilings do not imply identical action counts or intervention utility.
